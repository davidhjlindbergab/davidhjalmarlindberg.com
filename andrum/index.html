One dial, and the proof beside it.

Turn the dial to how clear the voice should be. Andrum makes the smallest cut that gets it there, and shows you how clear the voice is with Andrum and without it.

  1. Put Andrum on the music.

    A pad, the guitars, a beat, the music bed under a film: whatever should make room.

  2. Let it hear the voice.

    In a DAW, send the vocal to Andrum's sidechain; Andrum explains how in your DAW's own words. In Premiere Pro, Resolve or Final Cut Pro, put Andrum on the dialogue too and click This is the dialogue.

  3. Choose how clear the voice should be.

    80 % is a good start. Andrum makes the smallest cut that gets there, and tells you when it can't.

Only where the music hides the voice.

Every five milliseconds Andrum compares the voice with the music in 61 narrow bands. It works out how much of the voice the music masks, the way your ear would, and turns down only the bands where a cut brings the voice back.

Across the whole demo, Andrum hears the voice 41 % clear without it, and 84 % clear with it.

A model of the ear

The voice and the music are spread across the ear's critical bands, as masking spreads in the ear, and compared band by band: masked when the voice is 6 dB under the music, clear at 9 dB over. The bands where the voice carries most count most.

Only where a cut can help

Where the voice is buried too deep for any cut you allow to bring it back, Andrum leaves the music alone. Cutting there would cost the music and give the voice nothing.

At zero latency

61 filters a sixth of an octave apart, solved every 5 ms and gliding sample by sample. No delay for your DAW to make up for, and every cut lands within 0.64 dB of where Andrum means it.

Ask for clarity, not decibels.

A ducker cuts as deep as you set it, every time the voice comes in, whether the voice needs it or not. Andrum asks how clear the voice should be and cuts only what it takes. When the voice already comes through, the music is left alone.

What does the clarity percentage mean?

How much of the voice a listener can make out over the music, as Andrum's model of the ear measures it: 0 % is fully masked, 100 % fully clear. You choose the clarity you want on the dial, and Andrum shows what it reached with and without its cut.

Does Andrum change the voice?

No. Andrum only changes the track it's on. The voice it listens to passes through untouched.

Which apps does it work in?

Logic Pro, Ableton Live, FL Studio, Cubase, Studio One, Bitwig Studio, Reaper and GarageBand, and the video editors Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve and Final Cut Pro. Andrum comes as an Audio Unit and a VST3, plus a standalone app, and shows up in your plug-in list under David HJ Lindberg.

Does it work in Pro Tools?

Not yet. Pro Tools only loads AAX plug-ins, and Andrum isn't available as AAX yet.

Is there a Windows version?

Not yet. Andrum is for Mac today.

Does it add latency?

No, Andrum runs at zero latency. For a kick and a bass you can give it up to 30 ms of lookahead, which your DAW compensates for.

Is it heavy on my Mac?

No. With every feature on, Andrum runs about ten times faster than real time on one core of our test machine, and an instance whose inputs fall silent rests until they play again.

Is it only for vocals?

No. Anything can make room for anything: a kick through a bass, a snare through synths, a lead over chords, reverb and delay that step back under a vocal. There are 23 presets to start from.

What happens when the trial ends?

Andrum lets the sound through untouched until you add a licence. Nothing in your sessions breaks, and once you add a licence it works exactly as before.

Can I use it on more than one computer?

Yes. One licence covers one person on up to two Macs. When you get a new Mac, remove the licence from the old one in Andrum and add it on the new one.

Does it need the internet or an iLok?

No. Your licence is a short piece of text that Andrum checks on your Mac. Andrum never connects to the internet.
